## In short

A held note usually drifts for a reason you can change: the breath pressure under it falls as the air runs out, and the pitch sags with it. Keeping it steady means keeping that pressure even, ribs open and release slow, a coordination built with short, repeated holds, not a gift. A small, regular wave around one pitch is vibrato and is fine; a slow lean away is drift. Hold only while the end sounds like the start.

## Steps

### Use the practice material

Open the practice material on this page and choose Sustained hold or Soft sustain. Hear the reference, choose a comfortable key, then stop it before singing. The reading Breath: support versus pressure is listed there too.

- Look: The exercise, song or chapter is open. Your chosen range remains comfortable.
- Listen: Synthesized pitches supply the notes; they are not recordings of a singer demonstrating a technique.

### Set a safe baseline

Use a comfortable pitch and moderate volume. Let the reference stop before singing, and choose a duration you can finish without running empty.

- Look: Posture, room, device, and the planned stop rule are settled before the first attempt.
- Listen: Begin from an easy, repeatable sound; silence playback before judging your own voice.

### Name the target: Sustained tone

Listen for drift on a short, comfortable hold and change one thing at a time. Say what will count as complete in this lesson before practicing, and keep the attempt inside the range and duration you can repeat comfortably.

- Look: Keep the supplied reference or reading visible, settle your posture, and use only a pitch and duration you can repeat comfortably.
- Listen: The tone does not sag at the end, pulse with changing air pressure, or become louder to compensate for lost steadiness.

### Hear the distinction

Hear Sustained hold once and stop the reference. Then, in the [recorder](/recorder), record two holds on the same comfortable note in one take, with a quiet breath between them: the first for a slow count of eight, the second for a count of four. The length is the only thing that changes. Play both back and compare how each one ends: if the longer hold sags flat or thins at the end while the shorter one ends on the pitch it started on, practice at the shorter length and add a count only once its end matches its start. Aim for one centered pitch and an even release. Judge it by ear, not by the display.

- Look: Keep the supplied reference or reading visible, settle your posture, and use only a pitch and duration you can repeat comfortably.
- Listen: The tone does not sag at the end, pulse with changing air pressure, or become louder to compensate for lost steadiness.

- Look: Note the pitch, key, or setup you used so that your next comparison is useful.

## If it goes wrong

- The target disappears when the range, volume, speed, or duration increases. => If the note drifts, shorten it, reset the breath, and correct one direction on the next attempt. Do not chase every frame while still singing.
- The reference and your own sound seem different, or you cannot judge the result. => Treat the reading as missing, not as a pass or failure. Stop reference audio, restore a quiet setup, make one easier fresh attempt, and keep the human listening judgment separate from the measurement.

## Practice blocks

- 30s: Open the practice material. Prepare the room, body, and safe baseline for “Steady Is a Skill, Not a Talent.”
- 45s: Isolate the sustained tone target in short, comfortable examples.
- 60s: Practice or record the complete “Steady Is a Skill, Not a Talent” task, resetting between attempts.
- 45s: Review one take against the stated listening goal and write the next smaller correction.
