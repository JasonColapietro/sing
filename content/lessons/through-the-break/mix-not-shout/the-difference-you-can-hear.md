## In short

A shout gets its size from more air and more squeeze at the folds, so as it climbs it turns tight, goes sharp or cracks, and it tires the voice. Mix is the middle ground between chest and head voice, part fold setup and part vowel and resonance, where the transition can be crossed without a seam, and a belt carries a chest-like sound higher by getting brighter rather than by pushing. The difference you can hear is in repetition: an easy call sounds the same the third time, a shout costs more each time, and any grip in the throat means stop and go lighter.

### Set a safe baseline

Work only after an easy warm-up, on a pitch below the top of your comfortable range. Keep attempts short and have a clear stop rule before starting.

- Look: Posture, room, device, and the planned stop rule are settled before the first attempt.
- Listen: Begin from an easy, repeatable sound; silence playback before judging your own voice.

### Name the target: Comfortable mixed-voice coordination

Belt and shout sound similar for about two seconds. Then one of them stops. Say what will count as complete in this lesson before practising, and keep the attempt inside the range and duration you can repeat comfortably.

- Look: The chin stays level, the jaw remains available, and the pitch does not require a raised chest or rigid neck.
- Listen: The tone stays clear and repeatable for the planned short hold, with an easy release and no rasp that persists afterward.

### Hear the distinction

Say “hey!” as if calling a friend across a quiet room — a call, not a shout. Sing that “hey” on a comfortable note below your transition, then move it up one semitone at a time only while it stays as easy as speaking; if it starts to need a push, let it lighten instead. Record the climb in one take. Pitch is the only thing that changes, so on playback listen for the first note where the call starts to sound like a shout: harder, louder without getting brighter, or tight. That note is where to lighten next time. Never chase a belt label by adding throat pressure.

## If it goes wrong

- The target disappears when the range, volume, speed, or duration increases. => Stop immediately for pain, burning, sudden hoarseness, loss of range, or effort that lingers. Resume another day or work with a qualified teacher; do not repeat to prove toughness.
- The reference and your own sound seem different, or you cannot judge the result. => Treat the reading as missing, not as a pass or failure. Stop reference audio, restore a quiet setup, make one easier fresh attempt, and keep the human listening judgment separate from the measurement.

## Practice blocks

- 30s: Open the practice material. Prepare the room, body, and safe baseline for “The Difference You Can Hear.”
- 60s: Isolate the comfortable mixed-voice coordination target in short, comfortable examples.
- 90s: Practise or record the complete “The Difference You Can Hear” task, resetting between attempts.
- 60s: Review one take against the stated listening goal and write the next smaller correction.
