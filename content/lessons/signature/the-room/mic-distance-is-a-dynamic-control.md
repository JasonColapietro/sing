## In short

A microphone hears distance as loudness: move from a hand's width to arm's length and it hears a different instrument. That makes distance a control: keep a quiet line close and ease back for a big note instead of pushing the quiet line or squeezing the loud one. Too close and loud notes distort; too far and quiet ones vanish. Hearing yourself matters as much: if the monitors let you down, you sing louder by reflex. Sort that out first.

### Hear the distinction

Sing the first phrase of Amazing Grace twice at the same comfortable volume: once with the microphone (or phone) a hand's width from your mouth, once at arm's length, angled slightly off to the side so your breath does not hit it. Change only the distance. Leave a quiet gap while you move it so handling noise stays separate, and record both in one take in the recorder. On playback, note which sounds closer and fuller, and whether either distorted on the loudest note or lost the quietest one. Then sing the phrase once using distance on purpose: close for the quiet opening, easing back a little for the fullest note.

## If it goes wrong

- The target disappears when the range, volume, speed, or duration increases. => If a loud note distorts or a quiet one disappears on playback, keep your volume the same and move the microphone instead: a little further back for the fullest phrase, closer for the quietest, then record that phrase again. If you add a harmony and it collapses into the melody, stop the reference, sing the harmony alone from its anchor notes, then bring the melody back at lower volume.
- The reference and your own sound seem different, or you cannot judge the result. => Treat the reading as missing, not as a pass or failure. Stop reference audio, restore a quiet setup, make one easier fresh attempt, and keep the human listening judgment separate from the measurement.
