A run is the moment in a song when one syllable turns into eight notes. Done well, it sounds like the singer simply let go and the notes fell out in order. That impression is the result of a lot of very unglamorous work, almost all of it slow.

This chapter goes with the program called Riffs and runs in 4 weeks. It builds on chapter 9, which explained what a pitch score measures, and on the agility work in the free Warmups room.

## What a run is made of

Take any run you admire and slow the recording down. You will find it is not a smear. It is a short string of distinct pitches, and those pitches are almost always drawn from one of three small sets.

The first is the major pentatonic scale: five notes, the major scale with its fourth and seventh left out. Count them as 1, 2, 3, 5 and 6. With no half-steps in it, nothing in the scale clashes, which is why a run built on it sounds settled.

The second is the minor pentatonic scale: 1, flat 3, 4, 5 and flat 7. It is the same five-note idea with a darker color, and it is the backbone of most blues, rock and R&B runs.

The third is the blues scale, which is the minor pentatonic with one extra note squeezed in between 4 and 5. That flat five is a passing note. Singers lean on it for a moment and move on.

The technical name for many notes on one syllable is melisma. A riff is a short, memorable one that a singer may repeat. A run is usually longer and travels further. The words overlap, and nobody will correct you for using either.

Runs are also built from small repeated shapes. Call each shape a cell. A three-note cell falls 3-2-1. A turn circles a note: up, back, down, back. A triplet is three notes in the time of one beat, and runs made of falling triplets are the sound of a great deal of gospel. Learn six or seven cells and you can hear most runs as cells joined end to end.

## Why slow is the method

A fast run is a slow run that has been sped up. If the slow version has a wrong note in it, the fast version has the same wrong note, just harder to hear. Worse, practicing fast before the notes are placed teaches your voice an approximate shape, and an approximate shape is what a sloppy run is.

So the rule for the whole four weeks is that speed comes last. Sing a cell slowly enough that every note is a separate, deliberate pitch. Only when it scores well three reps in a row do you bring the tempo up. The Warmups room has a tempo control under Adjust. Use 0.75× without embarrassment, and use it for longer than feels necessary.

There is a physical reason too. A run is fast because the pitch changes are small and light, not because anything is working hard. Singers who push for speed add volume, and volume adds weight, and weight is slow. The quick exercises in this program should feel smaller than the slow ones.

## How the four weeks are built

Week 1 is free and uses only the free exercises. Day 1 takes a range test after a small three-note climb and a five-note scale. The other days sing slow scales and the free pentatonic run, then a chromatic neighbor and a minor scale for half-step accuracy, then a first try at the agility run. It is a week of placing notes.

Weeks 2 to 4 repeat one week of four day types.

"Cells, slow" is the Quick warmup and then the Riffs and runs pack. The pack has eight exercises and it is ordered from slow to quick: a three-note cell, a pentatonic climb, a minor pentatonic fall, a turn and fall, a blues cell, falling triplet cells, a seven-note riff, and a quick pentatonic scale up and back. The first five are at a tempo where you can think about each note. The last three are not, which is why they are last.

"Accuracy, then speed" swaps the Quick warmup for the free Agility and runs routine, which ends on fast five-note scales, and then sings the pack.

"Runs on syllables" is the day that most singers skip in their own practice and should not. It sings the Clear words pack, then the triplet cells and the quick pentatonic again. A run in a song is sung on a vowel, often after a consonant, and the consonant has to get out of the way without knocking the first note flat.

"The everyday set, then cells" uses the longer Daily warmup before the pack.

There is a rest day on day 4 and day 7 of every week, and every Pro day ends on the cool-down. The last day repeats day 1's warm-in and range test, then sings the pack once more.

## Three faults, and what fixes them

**Sliding.** The notes blur into one another, so the run sounds like a siren with bumps. The fix is a tiny pulse of breath on each note, not a hard restart. Sing the cell staccato once, with a clear gap after each note, then legato again. The staccato pass reminds the voice where each pitch is.

**Rushing the end.** The first three notes are in time and the rest tumble. This is nearly always a breath that was spent too early. Take a normal breath, not a big one, and think of the last note as the destination. A run is a phrase, and it needs a plan for its air like any other phrase.

**A flat bottom.** The run lands a little under its last note. Falling patterns invite this, because the voice relaxes on the way down and the pitch relaxes with it. Hear the landing note before you start, and aim slightly above where it feels natural.

## What the app scores, and what it cannot

Every exercise scores how close each note landed, at the tempo you set. Chapter 9 explained what that number is: the share of the exercise's scored time you spent inside a window of roughly half a semitone either side of the note. At slow tempos it is a fair report of whether you placed the notes.

At quick tempos, read it more gently. Each note lasts a quarter of a second or less, and any time spent arriving at a note is time outside its window, so a well-sung quick run can score below a well-sung slow one. Compare a quick exercise with its own earlier scores, never with a slow exercise.

What the app cannot hear is whether a run sounds like music. Timing against a beat, the lean on the first note of each triplet, where you get louder and where you pull back: all of that is style, and it is yours to judge. The recorder is the tool. Record the seven-note riff once a week, and listen for whether you can count the notes on playback.

## When to stop

Runs are light work when they are sung lightly. If your throat is tired after a runs session, you were singing them too loud or too high. Stop if anything scratches or hurts, and follow chapter 6 for everything else. The quick exercises are capped at eight reps each for a reason: past that, accuracy drops and you start practicing the mistakes.

## After the four weeks

You will not have a library of runs. You will have the pieces, and the habit of slowing things down, which is the part that lasts. To learn a specific run from a record, slow the recording, write the notes as scale numbers, find the cells, and sing it at half speed for a week before you try it in tempo.

## Try this

Open the [Three-note cell](/warmups?exercise=runs-three-note-cell) in Warmups and sing it, 3-2-1, on "no" at 0.75×. Count the notes in your head as you go. Then sing it at full tempo. If you can no longer count three separate notes, the full tempo is not ready yet, and that is the whole lesson of this chapter in six notes.
