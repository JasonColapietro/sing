Singers who want higher notes at least know they are asking for something difficult. Singers who want lower notes usually think it should be easy, since the notes are right there under the ones they speak on. Then they try to sing them and get a mutter, a rattle, or nothing.

This chapter goes with the program called Low notes in 3 weeks. Chapter 18 already covered the bottom of the range as part of the twelve-week plan, and everything in it still applies. This chapter is for the singer who wants three weeks on that one skill.

## What happens at the bottom

As pitch falls, the folds shorten, thicken and slacken. A slack fold needs very little air pressure to vibrate, and it does not tolerate much. Push more air at a low note and it does not get bigger. It gets rough, or it collapses into a rattle.

That rattle is vocal fry: the folds so slack that they open and close in slow, uneven pulses. It is a real register and speakers use it all the time. It is not a sung pitch, the app cannot score it, and the range test does not count it. Your lowest sung note is the lowest one that still has a clear, steady tone.

So the bottom of the voice is a place where less is the only thing that works. Chapter 18 put it in three words: low notes are made by release.

## The reach, and how to spot it

When a note is low, people reach down for it. The jaw drops further than the vowel needs. The chin tucks toward the chest. The larynx is pressed down by hand, as it were, to manufacture depth. From the inside it sounds dark and impressive. On a recording it sounds woofy and quieter than the note above.

Three checks will catch it.

Look in a mirror. Your head should not move when the pitch does. If your chin dips on the low note, you are reaching.

Listen for the note above. If the low note is noticeably quieter and duller on playback than the note a tone higher, you have darkened it instead of singing it.

Speak afterwards. If your speaking voice feels heavy or tired after a low session, you were pressing. A pressed-down larynx rarely stings, which is why chapter 18 warned that it is deceptive.

## The floor is mostly anatomy

How low a voice goes depends chiefly on the length and mass of the folds, and those are fixed. Training can make the notes near your floor clearer, steadier and easier to start. It can make them available on a day when you are not perfectly rested. It does not usually add many notes below the floor, and for a lot of singers it adds none.

That is not a reason to skip the work. The difference between a low note you can touch and a low note you can start cleanly, hold for four seconds and put a word on is the difference between a note and a usable note. Chapter 7 called that the gap between your absolute range and your performable one, and at the bottom the gap is often three or four semitones wide. Closing it is what three weeks can do.

## How the three weeks are built

Week 1 is free and uses only the free exercises. Day 1 takes a range test after a hum and a descending five. The other days go down by steps on hums and a tongue trill, then slide down on sighs and soft lip trills, then come down from the top on a slow "hoo" and a reverse arpeggio. Nearly everything descends. Approaching a low note from above, on a slide or a scale, lets it arrive with no adjustment, where attacking it cold invites the reach.

One exercise that week starts in a quiet creak and rolls up into a clean note. That is the only place fry appears in the program, and it is there as an onset, for a second, to find a slack and easy start. The scored part is the clear note after it. Nothing here asks you to sing in fry, and no exercise in the Low notes pack uses it.

Weeks 2 and 3 are the Low notes pack. It has eight exercises: a slow five down on "oh", a falling triad, a long low hold, an octave drop that settles on the low note, a slow glide down a fifth, three slow steps in minor, a rocking tone, and a walk down the whole octave. Every one of them is kept to the lower part of your ladder, and all but the octave walk step down a half-step on each rep, so a set of eight reps drifts toward the bottom rather than hammering one pitch.

There are three kinds of day. One sings the pack after the Quick warmup. One sings it after the longer Daily warmup. And one, "Quiet sounds, then low holds", opens on the Easy recovery routine instead of a warmup, then sings the pack and four extra reps of the low hold. That day is the quietest of the week on purpose. Low notes like a voice that has not been asked for much.

There is a rest day on day 4 and day 7 of every week, and every Pro day ends on the cool-down. The last day is the check-in: it repeats day 1's warm-in and range test, then sings the pack once more.

## Three rules for every low rep

**Speaking level.** Sing low notes at the level you talk at. They carry less energy than high ones and they are supposed to be quiet. The big low notes on records are made by a microphone held close.

**Chin level, jaw loose.** Let the vowel decide how far the mouth opens. A small "oh" or a neutral "uh" keeps the tone gathered without any darkening by hand.

**Stop at gravel.** When a rung of the ladder turns to a rattle you did not choose, that rung is your floor for today. Stop that exercise there. Forcing the next semitone is how a low session turns into a rough voice the next morning.

## What the app scores, and what it cannot

Every exercise scores how close each note landed. Low in the voice, that number is informative in one particular way. The bottom register tends to go flat before it goes rough. A pitch that sags a few cents under the target on every descent usually means the note is under-supported, not out of reach: the breath has gone slack along with the folds. Keep the ribs wide to the end of the phrase, as chapter 4 described, and the sag often lifts.

The app cannot hear how a low note is made. Clear or gravelly, pressed or free, is yours to judge, and the recorder helps. Take one recording of the low hold in week 2 and one on the last day, and compare the sound of the note, not its pitch.

## When to stop

Chapter 6's list applies in full. Stop the session if anything scratches or hurts, or if your speaking voice starts to feel heavy. Take a day off if you wake up hoarse. A voice that hurts or stays rough needs a doctor who can examine the vocal folds, such as an ENT or laryngologist.

One more caution, specific to this program. Do not practice low notes first thing in the morning and take the result as progress. Many voices sit lower and feel thicker in the morning, as chapter 8 noted, and loosen over the following hours. Those notes are borrowed. Chapter 12 explained why a reading like that tells you about the morning and not about your voice.

## After the three weeks

If your lowest note on the last day is the same as on day 1, read the rest of the result before you decide nothing happened. Can you start the notes just above it without a creak? Do they hold their pitch? Can you sing a word on them? Those are the changes this program is built for.

If a song you love still sits under your usable floor, chapter 21 has the honest answer. Moving the key up two semitones costs nothing, and nobody in the audience will know.

## Try this

In the [pitch studio](/studio), speak the word "oh" in your ordinary voice, then sing it on the same pitch and hold it for three seconds. Slide down three semitones from there without moving your chin, and stop the moment the tone changes. The note just above where it changed is your usable floor today.
