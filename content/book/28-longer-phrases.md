Every singer has a line that beats them. It is two bars longer than their breath, the place to breathe is in the middle of a word, and by the last note there is nothing left but a wheeze. The usual response is to take a bigger breath next time. It rarely helps, and this chapter is about why.

This chapter goes with the program called Longer phrases in 4 weeks. It rests on chapter 4, which explained support, and on the second half of chapter 18, which introduced phrase work in the twelve-week plan.

## Capacity is not the problem

An untrained adult can hold a steady note for something like fifteen to twenty-five seconds. Very few phrases in any song are that long. If you are running out of air on an eight-second line, you are not short of lung. You are spending the breath faster than the line needs.

There are three common ways to overspend.

The first is the opening. Singers let a puff of air escape before the first note starts, or begin the phrase louder than it needs to be. Chapter 15 worked on the start of a note, its onset. A breathy onset spends a good part of the breath before a single pitch has sounded.

The second is leakage. A tone with air in it, the breathy sound some styles use on purpose, spends air far faster than a clear one. Used for a whole verse, it shortens every phrase in the verse.

The third is the collapse. The ribs fall in the moment the phrase starts, so the breath arrives as one large push at the front and a trickle at the back. Support, in chapter 4's sense, is keeping the ribs wide so the air is released at an even rate.

A bigger breath fixes none of these. It also raises the pressure under the folds at the start of the phrase, which makes the first note harder to start gently, which makes the opening leak worse.

## A phrase is a plan

Chapter 18 drew a line between a sustain and a phrase. A sustain is one note held. A phrase is a musical sentence: several notes, usually with words, sung on one breath and shaped so it goes somewhere.

Phrases are planned. Singers and conductors pencil a breath mark, a small comma above the staff, at each place they intend to breathe. You should do the same on a lyric sheet. Decide before you sing where the breaths are, take them where you decided, and do not take them anywhere else. An unplanned breath is nearly always a gasp, and a gasp is high, shallow and noisy.

A planned breath is the opposite. It is low, quiet and no bigger than the phrase needs. Chapter 4 put the ordinary breath at comfortably full, about three-quarters, taken in silently. For a four-second line, less than that is plenty.

## How the four weeks are built

This is the one skill program with a second measurement. Day 1 takes a range test after a lip-trill arpeggio and a five-note scale, and then one sustain test, which times how long you hold a single note. The last day does exactly the same, so the two readings can be compared.

Week 1 is free and uses only the free drills and exercises. One day opens with a minute of box breathing and sings one long line at a time. One runs the counted exhale, the Farinelli drill, and then two slow holds. One sings three five-note passes on a single breath, then the swell and fade.

Week 2 alternates two days. "Breath, then the long-phrases pack" opens with box breathing and a lip trill, then sings the whole pack. "A counted breath, then four long lines" opens with the Farinelli drill and the Quick warmup, then sings four of the pack's exercises for five reps each.

The Long phrases pack has eight exercises, and each is a pattern of roughly seven to fourteen seconds meant for one breath: a scale then an arpeggio; three passes, each wider; a nine-note scale; a slow climb where every note is nearly a second long; a chain of three arpeggios; three long notes joined; thirds in sequence; and a slow minor octave. Each tip states the breath plan.

Weeks 3 and 4 add consonants. On two days a week the Words on pitch pack follows the warmup, and the three wider passes follow that. There is a reason for the order. A line of pure vowel is the easy case. Real phrases have words in them, and every consonant either stops the air or lets some escape. A singer with sloppy diction leaks breath on every "h", "f" and "s". Crisp, quick consonants are not only clearer. They are cheaper.

There is a rest day on day 4 and day 7 of every week, and every Pro day ends on the cool-down.

## Singing the long lines

**Start softer than you think.** The first two notes of a long pattern set the rate of spend for the whole thing. Medium-soft at the start leaves room to grow.

**Do not top up.** In the middle of a pattern, the urge to sneak a breath is strong. Sing through it. If you truly run out, stop, breathe, and start the rep again. A phrase with a sneaked breath in it is two short phrases, and you already know how to sing those.

**Finish the last note.** The end of the phrase is where pitch sags, because the pressure is falling. Keep the ribs out through the final note and let it end by stopping, not by fading into air. That clean ending is the release.

**Legato first.** Join the notes. Each restart between notes spends a little air, and thirteen notes means twelve chances to waste it.

## What the app scores, and what it cannot

Every exercise scores how close each note landed. That says something about the phrase, though not why. A note you dropped in order to breathe scores as missed, and a final note that sagged scores as flat. If the last notes of a long pattern keep scoring worse than the first ones, the breath plan is the first thing to check, with the mirror and the recorder, since the app cannot tell a breath that ran out from any other cause.

The app does not hear the breath itself. It cannot tell a low, quiet inhale from a gasp, and it does not know whether your ribs stayed wide. That part is yours to judge. The mirror is useful here: shoulders that rise on the inhale are the plainest sign of a high breath.

The sustain test gives you seconds. Treat the number carefully. It measures one clear note at one comfortable pitch, which is the simplest case, and it moves around by a few seconds from day to day for reasons that have nothing to do with training. Chapter 11 explained how to read a number like that. A week 4 reading a little above week 1 is consistent with progress. It is not proof of it, and a lower reading on a tired day is not proof of the opposite.

## When to stop

Breath work can make you light-headed. If you feel dizzy during box breathing or the counted exhale, stop, sit down and breathe normally. Come back to it later with smaller breaths. Dizziness means you were over-breathing, and bigger is the wrong direction.

Long phrases are light on the throat when they are sung at a medium level. If your voice is tired afterwards, you were probably pressing to squeeze out the last notes. End the phrase earlier next time. Everything in chapter 6 about pain and hoarseness applies as usual.

## After the four weeks

Take the line that beats you and mark it up. Find the consonants that leak. Decide the breath marks. Sing it at a medium level, softer at the start than seems right, and record it. Most lines that felt two bars too long turn out to be one leaky opening too long.

## Try this

Open [Scale, then arpeggio](/warmups?exercise=phrase-scale-twice) in Warmups and sing it on one breath at a medium level. Then do it again and start a little softer. If the second attempt ends with air to spare, you have just found the cheapest way there is to make a phrase longer.
