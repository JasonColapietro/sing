Belt is the sound most people mean when they say a singer has a big voice: the chorus that sounds like someone calling across a street, on pitch, for eight bars. It is also the sound most people hurt themselves chasing, because from the outside it looks like volume, and volume looks like effort.

This chapter goes with the program called Belt in 6 weeks. It does not replace chapters 2, 4, 6 and 10, and it leans on all four. If you have not read chapter 6, read it before day 8. The rules about when to stop are in there, and they apply here more than anywhere else in the book.

## What a belt is

Chapter 2 described chest voice as the thicker, shorter fold setup you speak in, and chapter 10 described the belt ceiling as the pitch where that setup stops being available. A belt is chest-dominant sound carried above the pitch where the voice would rather thin out, held together by the shape of the throat and mouth rather than by force from the lungs.

Two things in that sentence matter.

The first is that a belt is a call. Think of the way you would shout "hey" to a friend who is about to step off a curb. You do not take a huge breath first. You do not push. The sound is bright, forward and a little brassy, and it carries because of that brightness, not because of the air behind it. Teachers call the brightness twang. It comes from narrowing the space just above the folds, and it is the reason a small person can be heard at the back of a hall.

The second is that the breath does less than you expect. Chapter 4 made the case that more air makes tone worse. In a belt it also makes the note unstable and the throat sore. A good belt uses a moderate breath held back steadily by the ribs and the abdomen, and lets the brightness do the carrying. If you finish a belted phrase with your shoulders up and no air left, the phrase was pushed.

## What it is not

It is not the top of your range. Head voice goes higher, and the range test reports the higher figure. Your belt lives in a band from somewhere around the middle of your range to your belt ceiling, and for most voices that band is narrower than they would like.

It is not grit. Rasp, growl and distortion are separate effects, layered on top of a clean sound by singers who have the clean sound first. Nothing in this program teaches them, and nothing in this book does.

And it is not a volume. A belt at a medium level is still a belt. Most of the six weeks happens at a level you could keep up for a whole rehearsal.

## How the six weeks are built

Week 1 is free and uses only the free exercises. Day 1 takes a range test after a lip-trill arpeggio and a five-note scale. The rest of the week finds an easy, steady middle: sustained holds, the swell and fade, short detached notes on a staccato pattern. None of it is loud. You are locating the part of your range where a strong sound will later sit.

Weeks 2 and 3 are the Belt prep pack. It opens at speaking pitch with a bratty "nay" on one repeated note, moves to a "hey" on falling thirds, then short "bah" bursts, a "yah" triad, and two holds. Every one of those drills is capped to the lower middle of your range. That is deliberate. A strong hold on a high note is the version to build toward, not the one to repeat on every rung of the ladder. One day a week adds the Mix builder after the calls, so the bright sound gets carried across the middle while it is fresh. One day uses the longer Daily warmup first. Every day ends on the cool-down.

Weeks 4 to 6 add two things. The "Longer holds" day follows the pack with six more reps each of the forte "ah" hold and the fifth hold. The "Leaps into a strong note" day swaps the pack for Leaps and intervals, then adds the fifth hold and the octave-drop "hey". A leap is how a belt usually arrives in a song: the melody jumps a fourth or a fifth onto the long note of the chorus. Landing on that note without a scoop, at the pitch and in the bright sound, is the skill.

There is a rest day on day 4 and day 7 of every week. Take them. The last day repeats day 1's warm-in and range test, then sings the pack once more.

## What the app scores, and what it cannot

Every exercise scores how close each note landed to its target. That is the whole measurement. The app does not hear loudness as a level, it cannot tell chest voice from head voice, and it has no idea whether your throat is tight. A belt that scores 95 can be pushed, and a belt that scores 70 can be easy and simply a little sharp.

So use the score for the one thing it is good at here. Pitch is the first thing to go when a belt is forced: the note creeps sharp as the pressure rises, or wobbles as the setup gives way. If your accuracy on the holds drops from one rep to the next while the ladder is still low, you are working too hard for where you are. Everything else stays your ear's job, and the recorder's. Record the fifth hold once a week and listen the next day, when you are no longer inside the sound.

## When to stop

These are not suggestions.

Stop the rep if your neck tightens, your chin lifts, or the sound turns rough. Back off the level and try again lower on the ladder.

Stop the session if anything tickles, scratches or hurts, or if a note that was easy ten minutes ago now needs a run-up. Stop singing, including the sung cool-down, and rest your voice.

Take the next day off if you wake up hoarse, or if your speaking voice feels thick. Hoarseness the morning after belting can be a warning of vocal irritation or overuse. It does not mean you are building strength. When you come back, repeat a day from weeks 2 and 3 at a lower level before you go on.

And see a doctor who can examine the vocal folds, such as an ENT or laryngologist, if a rough voice hangs around, if there is pain after you stop, or if anything in chapter 6's list turns up. A seventh week of belting is the wrong answer to all of those.

## What six weeks can and cannot do

You may finish with a belt ceiling a semitone or two higher than it was. You may finish with exactly the same ceiling. Both are ordinary. What should change is the cost: the same notes, at the same level, for less effort, with the pitch steadier at the end of a long hold than it was in week 2.

That is worth more than a higher note. A chorus is rarely one high note. It is the same strong sound eight or sixteen times, and the singer who can do the sixteenth like the first is the one who gets through the set in chapter 23.

If you want the note above your ceiling, chapter 17's route is still the right one: head voice first, then a little weight added to it. Belt and mix meet up there, and the Mix in 4 weeks program is the natural one to run after this.

## Try this

At speaking pitch, call "hey" on one note as if to someone across a room, three times, with a normal breath. Then open [Belt prep](/warmups?routine=belt-prep) in Warmups, sing its first exercise and compare. If the sung version feels like more work than the spoken call, bring the level down until the two match. That matched effort is the starting point for all six weeks.
