Most singers have a head voice they do not trust. It turns up by accident on a yawn or a laugh, it sounds thin next to the voice they speak in, and it cracks when they lean on it. So they avoid it, and spend years trying to get chest voice to do a job it was not built for.

This chapter goes with the program called Head voice in 4 weeks. The physical account is in chapter 2, and the reason the program insists on quiet is in chapter 17. Here is how the two fit a month of practice.

## What head voice is

As pitch rises, the folds lengthen and thin, and less of their depth takes part in each vibration. That lighter setup is what teachers call head voice. The name comes from where singers feel it, not from where it is made. It is made in the same place as every other note you sing.

Falsetto is the neighbor people confuse it with. In falsetto the folds do not close fully, so the sound is breathy and soft-edged and runs out of air quickly. In head voice they do close, so the sound is clear, it can swell and fade, and it connects downward without a gap. Chapter 10 gave you the way to tell them apart by ear: head voice can crescendo without falling apart, and falsetto, pushed for volume, breaks or flips down. So the quickest check is a swell. Sing a high note quietly and make it gradually louder. If it grows, that is head voice. If it stays the same size and then flips, that was falsetto.

Neither is wrong. Falsetto is a color some styles are built on. But head voice is the one that can be joined to the rest of your range, and joining is the point of this program.

The app cannot tell them apart. It hears pitch. Which of the two you are making is yours to judge, and the swell test is how you judge it.

## Why light comes first

The instinct with a weak register is to strengthen it by singing it louder. In head voice that backfires. Louder means more air pressure, more air pressure makes the folds grip harder to resist it, and the grip is exactly the heavy setup you were trying to leave behind. The note either cracks back into chest or locks into a shout.

So the program runs the other way. For four weeks the upper notes are sung at a level you could talk over. Quiet takes away the option of forcing. It also shows you the truth: a head voice that only works when you blow at it is not there yet, and a head voice that speaks at a quiet level will take more later without complaint.

Light does not mean breathy. A quiet note still needs the folds to close and the breath to stay steady under it, in the sense chapter 4 gave the word support. If you can hear air in the tone, think of a small, clear "oo" or "ee" and bring the note forward until the air noise goes.

## How the four weeks are built

Week 1 is free and uses only the free exercises. Day 1 takes a range test after a hum and a small siren. The other days are hums and small sirens, then a light "gee" to the octave and a slow "hoo" descent, then a reverse arpeggio that starts on its top note and a soft held note a fourth up. Everything starts small. The descents matter most: starting high and coming down lets you arrive in the lighter setup instead of climbing into it.

Week 2 brings in the Head-voice builder pack after the Quick warmup. It has seven exercises: a hummed five down, a gentle siren to the fifth, a "wee" falling from the fifth, an "oo" siren to the octave, a "loo" arpeggio, octave leaps on "oo", and a top-note float. Two days that week use the longer Daily warmup first, so the pack is sung on a thoroughly warm voice.

Week 3 adds the High notes routine on two days, which goes past the octave to the tenth a half-step at a time. That routine fits its ladder to your measured range and never asks for a note above it.

Week 4 adds the long notes. The "Notes that hover" day follows the pack with six reps of the top-note float and four of the vibrato hold on the fourth. Those are the first long sustains above your speaking range, and they come last on purpose. A sustained note asks the setup to stay put for seconds at a time. By week 4 it can.

There is a rest day on day 4 and day 7 of every week. The last day is the check-in: it repeats day 1's warm-in and range test, then sings the pack once more. Every Pro day ends on the cool-down.

## The crack is information

Your voice will flip. Somewhere on the sirens, the sound will jump from one setup to the other with a small yodel. Everyone's does at first. The flip marks the spot where the two setups have not yet learned to hand over, and that spot is roughly your passaggio, which chapter 3 covered.

Do three things with it. Keep sliding, so the siren finishes. Get quieter as you approach the spot next time, not louder. And keep the vowel small and rounded, a soft "oo", letting it open a little toward "uh" as you go higher, the way chapter 5 described vowel modification.

Do not practice the crack by hammering it. Three slides through a rough spot is a useful rep. Fifteen is rehearsing the problem.

## What the app scores, and what it cannot

Every exercise scores how close each note landed. On the vibrato holds it also reports how fast and how wide the pitch moved. That is all. Nothing in the app hears register, breathiness or effort.

The scores are still worth watching in one specific way. Head voice that is being pushed tends to go sharp, and head voice that is under-supported tends to sag flat at the end of a hold. If the float hold drifts down in its last second, rep after rep, think steadier breath, not more of it. If the octave leaps overshoot, you are throwing weight at the top note.

For everything else, use the recorder. Take one short recording a week of the "loo" arpeggio at your quiet level. You hear your own voice partly through the bones of your skull, which favors the low, heavy part of the sound, so head voice seems thinner from the inside than it does to a listener. The recording is closer to the truth, and many singers like their head voice better on playback than they expected.

## When to stop

Chapter 6's rules hold. Stop the rep if you are reaching with your chin or if the sound turns rough. Stop the session if anything scratches or hurts, or if the top notes that were easy at the start now need a run-up. Take the day off if you wake up hoarse. A voice that hurts or stays rough is a question for a doctor who can examine the vocal folds, not for a warmup.

Head voice work is among the lightest loads in this book when it is done quietly. If a session leaves you tired, it was too loud.

## What four weeks can and cannot do

The program does not promise a higher top note. Some singers get one. What it is built to change is how the notes you already have behave: whether the octave leap lands the first time, whether the float holds its pitch for three seconds, whether the siren crosses the middle with a seam or without one.

Those are things you can hear on your week 1 and week 4 recordings, and they are what a song needs. When the four weeks are done, Mix in 4 weeks is the next step, because a head voice you trust is half of a mix.

## Try this

In the [pitch studio](/studio), sing the top note of an easy siren on a quiet "oo" and hold it. Swell it to a medium level over three seconds, then fade it back. If the note grows smoothly, you are in head voice. If it will not grow, or it flips, come down two semitones and try again there.
